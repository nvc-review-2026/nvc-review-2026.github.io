Advances in Neural Video Compression:
A Review and Benchmarking

Abstract

While conventional video coding standards remain predominant in real-world applications, neural video compression has emerged over the past decade as an active research area, offering alternative solutions with potentially significant coding gains through end-to-end optimization. Owing to the rapid pace of recent progress, existing reviews of neural video coding quickly become outdated and often lack a systematic taxonomy and meaningful benchmarking. To address this gap, we provide a comprehensive review of two major classes of neural video codecs—scene-agnostic and scene-adaptive—with a focus on their design characteristics and limitations. More importantly, we benchmark representative state-of-the-art methods from each category under common test conditions recommended by video coding standardization bodies. This provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first large-scale unified comparison between conventional and neural video codecs under controlled settings. Our results show that neural codecs can already achieve competitive, and in some cases superior, performance relative to VTM and AVM, although they still fall short of ECM in overall coding efficiency under both low delay and random access configurations.

Test conditions

Baselines

Six conventional codecs: VTM-20.0 (VVC reference software), VVenC/VVdeC (optimised VVC implementation), AV1, ECM and AVM (exploratory models beyond VVC and AV1 from MPEG JVET and AOM), and NVIDIA NVENC as a hardware encoder. All are run in low delay (LD) and random access (RA) configurations, following the JVET Common Test Conditions as closely as possible.

Neural codecs

We picked methods that are recent (published in the last three years), high-performing and reproducible (public code with pretrained models or training code).

  • Scene-agnostic, LD: MaskCRT, DCVC-DC, DCVC-FM, DCVC-RT, DCVC-UF (distortion-oriented); GVC-1D (perceptual).
  • Scene-agnostic, RA: B-CANF, BRHVC, DCVC-UF (HT-S), which uses a chunk-based structure with context carried across chunks rather than hierarchical B-frames.
  • Scene-adaptive: PNVC (LD and RA); C3, HiNeRV, NVRC and GIViC (RA). Apart from PNVC and GIViC, these share optimised parameters across the whole sequence rather than following a fixed GoP.
Datasets

UVG, MCL-JCV, HEVC Class B–E and AOM A2–A5. 4K/UHD sequences are excluded because of the compute and memory cost of most neural codecs.

Quality metrics

PSNR, MS-SSIM, VMAF, VMAF-NEG, LPIPS and DISTS (the last two are perceptual metrics). All distortions are measured in YUV 4:2:0. PSNR combines the channels as (6·PSNRY + PSNRCb + PSNRCr)/8 per frame. VMAF is computed on the MSE-optimised NVC models.

Bitrate and BD-rate

Bitrate comes from the full serialised bitstream: payload, headers, side information and any transmitted scene-specific model parameters. Shared pretrained models are assumed to be at the decoder already and are not counted. R = 8SF/T bit/s, where S is the size in bytes, F the frame rate and T the number of frames. BD-rate is computed per sequence against VTM-20.0 LD (the anchor for both LD and RA) and then averaged over each dataset. Curves are never pooled across sequences first.

Complexity and caveats

We report model size, kMAC/pixel and encoding/decoding FPS. Conventional codecs are timed on a dual-socket ARM Neoverse-V2 CPU and neural codecs on one NVIDIA A100 GPU, so the FPS numbers are not normalised across hardware. Two further caveats: DCVC-DC and DCVC-FM are numerically unstable on the 10-bit AOM A2–A5 sequences, and MaskCRT is trained in RGB, so it needs colour conversion.

Benchmark code

The scripts used for encoding, quality evaluation, BD-rate computation and complexity profiling are in benchmark/. See its README for the environment variables and how to run them.
